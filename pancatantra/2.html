Translation & Context

Prose Narrative:
As the tradition goes: In the southern country, there was a magnificent city named Mahilāropya. There reigned a king named Amaraśakti, thoroughly proficient in all sciences of polity, whose feet were continually adorned by the radiance of gems set in the diadems of bowed vassal kings, and who was a consummate master of all the sixty-four arts. He had three sons who were exceedingly dull-witted: Vasuśakti, Ugraśakti, and Anantaśakti. Observing them to be utterly averse to education, the King summoned his ministers and lamented: "Gentlemen, it is well known to you that these sons of mine are hostile to learning and devoid of all moral judgment. Looking upon them, even this thornless empire of mine brings me no joy. Rightly is it said—"

Verse 2 Translation:
English: "Of sons unborn, dead, or foolish, the dead and the unborn are far preferable. For those two cause sorrow but for a brief moment, whereas a foolish son torments his parents like a burning fire throughout their entire lifetime."

हिन्दी अनुवाद:
दक्षिण देश में 'महिलारोप्य' नामक एक प्रसिद्ध नगर था। वहाँ सभी नीतिशास्त्रों में निपुण, सामन्त राजाओं के मुकुट-मणियों की कान्ति से वन्दित चरणों वाले राजा अमरशक्ति राज्य करते थे। उनके वसुशक्ति, उग्रशक्ति और अनन्तशक्ति नामक तीन पुत्र हुए जो महामूर्ख थे। उन्हें विद्याविमुख देखकर राजा ने अपने मन्त्रियों को बुलाकर कहा—"आप सब जानते हैं कि मेरे ये तीनों पुत्र विद्या से विमुख और विवेकहीन हैं। इन्हें देखकर निष्कण्टक राज्य भी मुझे कोई सुख नहीं दे रहा है। ठीक ही कहा गया है—"
